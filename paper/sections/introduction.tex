\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Computational studies combine public data, fitted models, simulation engines, and automated analyses. Their conclusions are often described as predictive, reproducible, mechanistic, causal, or representative of a digital twin. These terms are not interchangeable. A model can predict held-out observations without identifying the process that generated them. An executable analysis can rely on a non-unique topology. A stable observational association can remain non-causal. The problem addressed in this work is how to encode these distinctions as executable knowledge and infer the strongest claim supported by a heterogeneous evidence package.

Mouse-brain modelling provides a demanding case. Atlases, tracer experiments, electrophysiology, connectomic reconstructions, response-prediction benchmarks, and simulation platforms operate at different scales \citep{oh2014mesoscale,melozzi2017virtualmouse,dai2020bmtk,billeh2020v1,willeke2022sensorium,bae2025microns}. Static or temporal prediction does not identify synaptic topology. A measured local synapse paired with a functional descriptor does not establish a whole-brain causal model. A brain-wide simulation based on inferred connectivity is not an entity-specific digital twin because it reproduces a macroscopic signal. Public availability and repeatable execution do not remove these evidence boundaries.

A global quality score cannot represent this situation reliably. Predictive correlation, cross-mouse agreement, matched structure--function differences, interventions, and clean software revisions have different meanings and units. Compensatory aggregation allows strength in one dimension to conceal a mandatory absence in another. Mechanistic wording should remain blocked when topology or direction fails, regardless of predictive performance. Causal wording requires intervention or an appropriate identification design, not reproducibility alone.

Knowledge engineering offers the required separation between a domain model, a fact base, an inference procedure, and an explanation \citep{buchanan1984rulebased,studer1998knowledge,guarino1998formal,lebo2013provo}. MouseClaimBench applies this structure to scientific claim authorization. A versioned profile defines ten mouse-brain claim types, their mandatory evidence blocks, and five prioritized rules. Evidence facts retain the original observations, executed predicate, rationale, source artifact, and revision. The engine returns supported, blocked, uncertain, out of scope, or referred for review together with the decisive rule and its witnesses. The suffix \emph{Bench} denotes the executable policy benchmarks shipped with the system rather than a dataset leaderboard.

The inference policy is non-compensatory and deterministic for a fixed profile and fact package. Failed mandatory evidence takes precedence over expert judgement, missing evidence, and protocol-scope states. Support requires all mandatory blocks to pass. Candidate discovery is outside the authorization path and cannot create evidence or modify a decision.

\paragraph{Research contributions}\label{sec:contributions}
The work provides four connected contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item a versioned, author-proposed mouse-brain claim profile with a machine-readable curation basis for every claim-to-evidence relation
\item a provenance-preserving fact model and deterministic non-compensatory inference engine that returns an operational explanation graph and complete proof trace
\item a known-truth benchmark with three decision policies, case-cluster uncertainty, and results disaggregated by claim and generating regime
\item artifact-grounded mouse-brain audits, substitution controls, and clean release checks whose admissible interpretations are explicitly bounded
\end{itemize}
These contributions define a reproducible claim-authorization method. They do not establish that the profile is a unique or community-validated scientific taxonomy.

Evaluation separates logical conformance from scientific evidence. It combines exhaustive rule tests, 500 known-truth structural-equation cases, four artifact-grounded cases, 40 author-generated migration decisions, and two substitution controls \citep{wadden2020scifact,mooij2016cause}. The non-compensatory policy obtains an \ac{fpr} of 0.019, compared with 0.050 for a compensatory baseline and 0.271 for a prediction shortcut. Positive real-case conclusions remain bounded to prediction, within-resource reproduction, topology specificity, and local observational structure--function association. No case supports causality, external biological replication, or a complete entity-specific mouse-brain digital twin.

The contribution is an evidence-constrained \ac{kbs}, not a neural simulator, predictive model, causal-discovery method, or automated reviewer. Only the computational mouse-brain profile is evaluated. Section~\ref{sec:related-work} defines the context, Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the method, and Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}--\ref{sec:conclusions} report the evaluation and conclusions.
