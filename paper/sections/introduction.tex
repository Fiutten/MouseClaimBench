\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\ac{ai} has moved from isolated prediction tasks towards systems that support scientific interpretation. Its outputs are now described as robust, reproducible, explanatory, mechanistic, causal, or representative of a digital twin. These terms are not interchangeable. A model can predict held-out observations and still fail to identify the process that generated them. An analysis can be reproducible and still be invalid for a causal statement. The central problem addressed in this paper is therefore not whether a computational result exists, but whether the scientific wording attached to that result is justified.

This problem is visible in neurocomputational modelling. Public atlases, connectomes, neural-response benchmarks, and large-scale simulators allow researchers to combine anatomical, functional, and computational information at unprecedented scale \citep{oh2014mesoscale,billeh2020v1,willeke2022sensorium,bae2025microns}. However, each resource observes a different part of the system and supports a different level of inference. Predictive agreement with neural activity does not establish a biological mechanism. A local structure--function association does not establish whole-brain causality. An executable whole-brain model does not become an entity-specific digital twin solely because it reproduces a macroscopic signal. Similar interpretation gaps arise in scientific fact checking and causal discovery, where retrieved evidence or statistical dependence can be mistaken for stronger support.

Existing practices address important parts of the problem. \ac{vv} relates model credibility to a defined context of use \citep{oberkampf2010verification,riedmaier2021vvuq}. Scientific claim verification links natural-language statements to documentary evidence \citep{wadden2020scifact,vladika2023survey}. Assurance cases organize claims, arguments, and supporting artifacts \citep{mohamad2021assurance,porter2024praise}. Reproducibility initiatives improve access to code, data, and execution details \citep{pineau2021reproducibility,barker2022fair4rs}. None of these contributions, in isolation, provides an executable rule that maps heterogeneous computational evidence to the strongest admissible wording of a scientific \ac{ai} result.

This missing link defines a \ac{dss} problem. The alternatives are editorial actions on a claim: retain it, weaken it, mark it as uncertain, remove it, or request external judgement. The evidence criteria are heterogeneous and cannot always compensate one another. In particular, strong prediction cannot replace absent causal evidence, and reproducibility cannot replace topology-specific validation. This resembles the veto logic of non-compensatory multicriteria decision aiding \citep{figueira2013electre}, but the decision object is a scientific statement and the evidence is bound to executable research artifacts.

We present ClaimBench, a claim-aware \ac{dss} that represents each scientific statement as an explicit contract. The contract declares the claim family, scope, mandatory evidence blocks, permitted wording, blocked wording, uncertainty policy, and artifact provenance. Authorization is conjunctive over mandatory evidence. ClaimBench also separates claim discovery from claim authorization. A deterministic extractor or an optional \ac{llm} can propose candidate statements, but neither can authorize them. The final decision is produced only from declared evidence and stored as an inspectable artifact.

The framework is evaluated through four complementary forms of evidence. A synthetic known-truth benchmark tests gate semantics across 144 adversarial cases. A 243-cell threshold grid and deterministic local perturbations expose sensitivity and uncertainty. SciFact and the Tuebingen Cause-Effect Pairs provide external controls for evidence-retrieval and causal-direction overclaiming \citep{wadden2020scifact,mooij2016cause}. Component ablations, manuscript auditing, threat modelling, and a clean release test determine whether the safeguards are operational and reproducible. The full gate produces no false authorizations in the known-truth suite, while correlation-only and compensatory alternatives produce measurable overclaiming risk. These results validate an operational decision rule under the tested conditions. They do not establish universal validity or automated peer review.

The contribution is a traceable evidence-to-claim decision layer instantiated for mouse-brain modelling and designed to remain transferable to other scientific \ac{ai} domains. ClaimBench is not a new predictor, simulator, causal-discovery algorithm, or fact-checking model. Its purpose is to prevent results from being promoted beyond the evidence that generated them and to make that boundary inspectable by authors and reviewers.
