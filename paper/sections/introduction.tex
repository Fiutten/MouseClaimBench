\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Scientific Artificial Intelligence (AI) is increasingly used to build models that are not only predictive, but also presented as mechanistic, causal, reproducible, or even digital-twin-like. This shift creates a methodological problem that is different from conventional model selection. A model may be useful as a predictor and still fail to support the scientific interpretation attached to it. A benchmark may show reproducible performance and still provide no evidence for topology-specific mechanisms. A manuscript may describe an experiment accurately in some places and overstate the same evidence in others. These are not cosmetic issues. They affect whether a computational result can be defended scientifically.

The problem is especially visible in neurocomputational modelling, where public resources, simulation packages, and predictive benchmarks have grown quickly. Datasets and tools such as large-scale connectomics, neural response prediction, and brain simulation frameworks make it technically possible to connect anatomical, functional, and computational evidence. At the same time, the distance between a useful model and a justified scientific claim remains large. A model that predicts neural activity does not automatically identify a mechanism. A graph that reproduces a structural pattern does not automatically imply causal dynamics. A local structure-function association does not imply a whole-brain digital twin. The same issue also appears outside neuroscience, for example in scientific claim verification and causal discovery benchmarks.

Current evaluation practice does not fully solve this problem. Predictive metrics evaluate output quality. Reproducibility checks evaluate whether the computation can be repeated. Verification and validation procedures evaluate whether a model is credible for an intended use. Scientific claim verification retrieves and classifies evidence for textual claims. Causal discovery benchmarks test whether direction can be inferred under controlled settings. These families of work are necessary, but they are usually treated as separate activities. As a result, a paper can pass a predictive benchmark and still overclaim causal interpretation, or it can include reproducible artifacts and still leave the relation between evidence and wording implicit.

Large Language Models (LLMs) make this gap more important. LLMs can help inspect a manuscript, extract candidate claims, and detect wording patterns that deserve audit. They can also introduce authority drift if their outputs are treated as validation. In a scientific AI pipeline, an LLM should not decide whether a claim is supported. That decision must remain tied to transparent evidence, explicit criteria, and reproducible artifacts. This paper therefore treats LLMs as a bounded assistance layer rather than as an evaluator.

We present ClaimBench, an executable decision-support framework for scientific AI claim governance. The central idea is simple. Claims are represented as contracts. Each contract states what type of claim is being made and which evidence dimensions are required before that claim can be authorized. The gate is non-compensatory. Strong prediction cannot compensate for missing causal evidence. Reproducibility cannot compensate for missing topology specificity. A manuscript cannot use digital-twin language unless the required evidence dimensions are present. This design is deliberately conservative because the target is not leaderboard performance. The target is defensible scientific wording.

The current implementation evaluates ClaimBench through synthetic known-truth cases, external controls, manuscript auditing, ablations, threat modelling, and a release check. The external controls are chosen to stress two common sources of overclaiming. SciFact is used as a scientific claim-auditing case, not as a state-of-the-art verification task. The Tuebingen cause-effect benchmark is used to expose causal-direction overclaiming, not to claim causal-discovery performance. A non-authoritative LLM-ready layer extracts candidate claims from a manuscript or from a local JSON file generated elsewhere, but claim authorization remains controlled by executable artifacts.

The contribution of this paper is therefore methodological and decision-support-oriented. ClaimBench does not introduce a new neural simulator, a new biological digital twin, a new SciFact model, or a new causal discovery algorithm. It contributes an auditable layer that connects claims to evidence under explicit failure modes. This makes it relevant for scientific AI projects where the main decision is not only whether the model works, but whether the resulting paper says more than the evidence supports.
