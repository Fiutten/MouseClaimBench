\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper introduced ClaimBench, an executable \ac{dss} for matching scientific \ac{ai} wording to heterogeneous computational evidence. Its central mechanism is a claim contract with mandatory evidence blocks. The resulting gate is non-compensatory, exposes uncertainty, binds decisions to artifacts, and keeps \ac{llm}-assisted claim discovery outside scientific authorization. The framework is instantiated for mouse-brain modelling, where predictive, structural, mechanistic, causal, and digital-twin interpretations frequently depend on evidence collected at different biological scales.

The evaluation establishes that this mechanism is operational under the declared conditions. Across 1,152 known-truth claim opportunities, the full gate produces no false authorization or false rejection. Correlation-only, leaderboard-only, reliability-only, and compensatory alternatives reintroduce measurable overclaiming or excessive conservativeness. The threshold grid identifies 108 safe and 135 dangerous configurations. SciFact and Tuebingen demonstrate that retrieval and correlation can remain unsafe bases for support and causal direction. The frozen package reproduces all 15 stages and passes its unified and threat-model checks.

These findings support a bounded contribution. ClaimBench makes the evidence-to-claim decision explicit, traceable, and reproducible. It does not establish universal claim validity, automated peer review, state-of-the-art fact checking, causal-discovery performance, or the biological validity of a complete mouse-brain model. Its synthetic labels encode operational semantics, its external controls diagnose selected failure modes, and its current claim extractor lacks an expert-annotated evaluation corpus.

The next decisive validation is human and external. A blinded multi-domain manuscript study should compare ClaimBench with deterministic baselines and fixed claim extractors using candidate recall, false authorization, abstention, inter-rater agreement, review time, and revision quality. A complementary neuroscience study should test whether independent reviewers make better calibrated wording decisions for mouse-brain claims when predictive, topology, direction, structure--function, and intervention evidence is presented through the contracts. These studies are required before claiming improved editorial decisions or general reviewer benefit.

Further technical work should estimate thresholds on development corpora and evaluate them on independently held-out studies. It should also define explicit false-authorization control at a declared coverage level, compare uncertainty procedures under their valid assumptions, and version contracts together with artifact hashes, data releases, environments, and authorized text spans. Domain profiles can subsequently extend the taxonomy to safety, fairness, clinical validity, deployment, or policy claims through expert elicitation and established assurance patterns rather than through an unrestricted hand-written list.

Until this evidence exists, ClaimBench should be used as a transparent pre-submission and review-support instrument. Its broader conclusion is deliberately narrow. Reproducible execution and strong prediction do not, by themselves, authorize mechanistic, causal, or digital-twin interpretations. A defensible scientific statement should identify the evidence that permits its wording, the scope in which that evidence applies, and the missing condition that would block a stronger claim.
