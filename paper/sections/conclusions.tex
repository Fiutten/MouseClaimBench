\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper presented MouseClaimBench, a non-compensatory \ac{dss} for connecting scientific wording with heterogeneous validation evidence in computational mouse-brain modelling. The method represents each candidate statement as a versioned contract, evaluates source-specific evidence without imposing a false common scale, and returns one of five auditable dispositions. Prediction, reproducible computation, internal reproduction, external replication, topology, direction, structure--function association, mechanism, causality, and digital-twin scope remain separate authorization targets. An optional \ac{llm} may discover candidate statements, but it cannot authorize them or create missing evidence.

The empirical result is bounded but substantive. Across 500 independently generated structural-equation cases, MouseClaimBench reduces the false-positive rate from $0.271$ for a predictive shortcut to $0.019$. It has fewer claim errors in 263 cases, compared with 77 for the shortcut, with 160 ties. Four artifact-grounded mouse cases demonstrate that the policy is not equivalent to universal rejection. It retains predictive and topographic evidence for Static Sensorium, temporal prediction for Dynamic Sensorium, internal reproduction for Allen, and a local observational structure--function association for \ac{microns}. At the same time, it blocks causal, mechanistic, externally replicated, and complete digital-twin interpretations when their mandatory evidence is absent or fails. SciFact and Tuebingen confirm that retrieval and association are unsafe substitutes for support and direction.

These results do not establish automated peer review or improved human decisions. The next study must execute the prepared double-blinded expert protocol after ethics review and preregistration. Its primary analysis should compare unaided and contract-assisted decisions using false authorization, false rejection, abstention, calibration, agreement, review time, and revision quality. Independent experts must establish the reference labels. A second validation should freeze the domain predicates before applying them to mouse-brain studies selected outside the current project. The \ac{microns} analysis should be extended to independent animals or resources and evaluated with variance estimators suitable for dyadic or graph-dependent observations.

Technical development should focus on calibrated selective decisions rather than a larger opaque score. Thresholds should be estimated on development studies and evaluated on held-out studies under an explicit false-authorization budget. Modern scientific claim verifiers and causal-discovery systems should be integrated as evidence producers, while the authorization boundary remains inspectable. Contract versions, artifact hashes, environments, authorized text spans, and reviewer overrides should be recorded in one provenance graph. Domain extensions should be produced through expert elicitation and established assurance practice rather than by reusing mouse-brain thresholds.

The main conclusion is precise. Reproducible execution and accurate prediction do not, by themselves, authorize mechanistic, causal, or digital-twin wording. MouseClaimBench makes that distinction executable and traceable. Its present contribution is a scientifically hardened validation layer and a reproducible basis for the human and external studies that are still required.
