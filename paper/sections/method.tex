\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

ClaimBench is designed as a decision-support process for authors, reviewers, and research teams. Its inputs are a manuscript, a set of declared scientific claims, and the computational artifacts that may support those claims. Its output is not a paper-level acceptance score. It is a claim-level decision with an evidential rationale, provenance, permitted wording, and blocked wording. Fig.~\ref{fig:claimbench-workflow} presents the complete workflow.

\subsection{Claim contracts and evidence representation}
\label{subsec:claim-contracts}

Let $c$ denote a candidate statement and let $k(c)$ denote its claim family. The current taxonomy contains predictive, reproducibility, evidence-retrieval, topology-specific, directional, structure--function, mechanistic, causal, digital-twin, and state-of-the-art claims. The taxonomy is operational. It captures recurring interpretation errors in scientific AI papers and does not attempt to define a general philosophy of scientific explanation.

A claim contract is represented as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}(c)=\langle k, s, R_k, \boldsymbol{\tau}_k, u, w^{+}, w^{-}, \mathcal{A}\rangle,
\label{eq:claim-contract}
\end{equation}
where $s$ is the declared scope, $R_k$ is the set of mandatory evidence blocks, $\boldsymbol{\tau}_k$ contains their operational thresholds, $u$ is the uncertainty policy, $w^{+}$ and $w^{-}$ are the permitted and blocked formulations, and $\mathcal{A}$ is the set of bound artifacts. Each artifact declaration contains a path and one or more expectations over stored fields. A contract therefore fails when a required file is missing, an expected decision is not reproduced, or a mandatory evidence block does not pass.

For a claim $c$, the evidence vector is $\mathbf{e}(c)=(e_1,\ldots,e_J)$. Its elements may be numerical, such as predictive agreement or a matched effect size, or logical, such as the availability of intervention evidence, independent validation, or a clean release. Every mandatory block is converted into a Boolean decision
\begin{equation}
b_j(c)=\mathbb{I}\bigl[e_j(c)\ \text{satisfies}\ \tau_{j,k}\bigr]\,\mathbb{I}\bigl[\mathcal{A}_j\ \text{passes}\bigr],
\label{eq:evidence-block}
\end{equation}
where the first indicator evaluates the declared criterion and the second evaluates artifact availability and expectations. This representation keeps numerical evidence and release provenance in the same contract without claiming that they measure the same construct.

Table~\ref{tab:claim-semantics} gives the principal claim classes and their mandatory evidence. Stronger claim families contain additional veto conditions. For example, a mechanistic claim requires predictive, reproducibility, topology, and direction blocks. A causal claim requires explicit causal evidence. Digital-twin wording additionally requires entity and scope conditions that are not satisfied by a local association or a generic population model.

\subsection{Non-compensatory authorization and uncertainty}
\label{subsec:claim-authorization}

ClaimBench authorizes claim family $k$ through a conjunctive rule
\begin{equation}
G_k(c)=\prod_{j\in R_k} b_j(c).
\label{eq:claim-gate}
\end{equation}
The claim is authorized only when every mandatory block equals one. Equation~\ref{eq:claim-gate} is the formal source of non-compensation. It differs from a weighted score $S(c)=\sum_j w_j e_j(c)$ because no increase in a passing block can offset a failed block. The rule resembles a multicriteria veto, although ClaimBench applies it to claim authorization rather than alternative ranking \citep{figueira2013electre}.

The operational thresholds are declared in the repository and exposed to sensitivity analysis. They are not universal scientific constants. The nominal implementation uses thresholds of $0.30$ for prediction, $0.70$ for reproducibility, $0.05$ for topology effect, $0.50$ for directed fraction, and $0.01$ for matched structure--function effect. Boolean requirements, including causal evidence and independent validation, are evaluated directly. The threshold grid reported in Section~\ref{subsec:known-truth-results} is part of the method because a gate that hides its dangerous configurations would provide false assurance.

ClaimBench adds an uncertain state through deterministic local perturbations. Let $\mathbf{e}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{e}^{(M)}$ be the nominal evidence vector and the declared perturbations around its numerical components. The authorization stability is
\begin{equation}
s_k(c)=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}G_k\bigl(c,\mathbf{e}^{(m)}\bigr).
\label{eq:claim-stability}
\end{equation}
The decision is supported when $s_k(c)\geq0.95$, blocked when $s_k(c)\leq0.05$, and uncertain otherwise. This score is a deterministic local stability proportion. It must not be interpreted as a posterior probability, a confidence level, or a distribution-free guarantee. Its purpose is to prevent a locally unstable authorization from being reported as established support.

Known-truth experiments permit two explicit error measures. For each evaluator, a false positive is an unsupported claim that is authorized and a false negative is a supportable claim that is blocked. The Overclaiming Risk Index (ORI) and Conservativeness Index (CNI) are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{ORI} &= \frac{FP}{FP+TN},
\label{eq:ori}\\
\mathrm{CNI} &= \frac{FN}{FN+TP}.
\label{eq:cni}
\end{align}
ORI measures the fraction of unsupported claim opportunities that are admitted. CNI measures the fraction of supportable opportunities that are withheld. Both are required because a gate can avoid overclaiming by blocking everything. In the present benchmark, lower values are better and the two indices are interpreted together.

\subsection{Artifact binding, claim discovery, and human authority}
\label{subsec:artifact-binding}

Claim contracts are stored in a small YAML schema. Each specification records the claim identifier, family, scope, evidence family, severity, permitted and blocked wording, blocked textual patterns, non-compensatory requirements, uncertainty policy, and required artifacts. The audit resolves each artifact path, reads structured outputs, evaluates the declared expectations, and records missing or failing evidence. This creates a reproducible link from manuscript wording to the exact code and data product used to justify it.

Claim discovery occurs before authorization. The reproducible mode uses a deterministic extractor that identifies candidate sentences associated with prediction, mechanism, causality, structure--function relations, reproducibility, generalization, digital twins, and state-of-the-art language. An optional local JSON file can contain candidates produced by an external LLM. The source of each candidate is recorded, but the candidate is treated as untrusted input. It cannot change thresholds, create evidence, or authorize a claim.

This separation addresses authority drift in LLM-assisted scientific workflows. Modern verification systems can improve retrieval, decomposition, and attribution, but their outputs remain sensitive to context and evidence quality \citep{fadeeva2024factchecking,tan2026scitrue}. ClaimBench uses language models only to increase inspection coverage. The final decision remains deterministic for a fixed evidence package and can be traced to the contract and artifacts.

The human retains authority at two points. First, domain experts define the contract scope and mandatory evidence. ClaimBench cannot determine that a domain-specific criterion is scientifically sufficient without that specification. Second, experts resolve claims marked as needing external review. The system supports editorial decisions but does not judge novelty, importance, ethical acceptability, or paper acceptance.

The complete process has five stages. Candidate statements are discovered and classified. Contracts bind each statement to mandatory evidence. Artifact checks evaluate metrics, controls, provenance, and release integrity. The non-compensatory gate returns supported, blocked, or uncertain status. Finally, the decision artifact links the result to bounded wording and the release state. This design makes wording drift detectable. A numerical result may remain unchanged while an unsupported interpretation is reintroduced during manuscript revision, and the manuscript audit is intended to catch that case.
