\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

ClaimBench intersects several mature research areas, but it does not duplicate any one of them. Its position is defined by the connection between a manuscript-level statement, heterogeneous computational evidence, a non-compensatory decision rule, and an executable release artifact. This section examines the closest areas and identifies the remaining methodological gap.

\subsection{Model credibility, assurance, and reproducibility}
\label{subsec:credibility-assurance}

Verification and validation (V\&V) distinguish whether a computational implementation solves the declared equations correctly from whether the resulting model is adequate for its intended use \citep{oberkampf2010verification,sargent2013verification}. Verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification frameworks extend this distinction by relating model credibility to available evidence, uncertainty, and context of use \citep{riedmaier2021vvuq,asme2018vv40}. These principles are directly relevant to scientific AI because repeatable execution does not imply validity for every interpretation. A predictor can be valid for interpolation and invalid for a mechanistic statement.

Assurance cases provide a second relevant foundation. They organize a high-level claim into subclaims, assumptions, argument strategies, and supporting evidence. The AI assurance literature maps a broad and fragmented collection of validation, verification, safety, and governance methods \citep{batarseh2021assurance}. Reviews of security assurance cases show that claim decomposition is well established, but concrete evidence assignment, maintenance, and tool support remain uneven \citep{mohamad2021assurance}. Recent work has adapted assurance arguments to AI ethics and autonomous systems, emphasizing traceability and the ability to challenge the evidence attached to a claim \citep{porter2024praise}. ClaimBench shares this concern for explicit claim-evidence relations. It differs by targeting the wording of empirical scientific AI papers and by evaluating contracts directly against repository artifacts.

Reproducibility and research-software initiatives address another necessary layer. The FAIR principles, FAIR research-software guidance, model cards, datasheets, and machine-learning reproducibility programmes improve access to data, code, intended use, and limitations \citep{wilkinson2016fair,mitchell2019modelcards,gebru2021datasheets,pineau2021reproducibility,barker2022fair4rs}. Biomedical modelling guidance has similarly moved from availability towards transparent, reusable, and executable computational practice \citep{patel2023fairbiors,sauro2026cure}. These contributions improve the quality of the evidence package. They do not determine whether a specific result authorizes mechanistic, causal, or digital-twin wording. ClaimBench treats reproducibility as one mandatory block, not as a substitute for scientific identification.

\subsection{Scientific claim verification and LLM-assisted evidence use}
\label{subsec:claim-verification}

Scientific claim verification usually formulates the task as document retrieval, evidence selection, and classification into support, refutation, or insufficient information. SciFact established a benchmark of expert-written claims linked to evidence-containing abstracts and rationales \citep{wadden2020scifact}. MultiVerS improved full-context verification through joint label and rationale prediction, while SciFact-Open demonstrated that systems trained on restricted corpora lose performance in open-domain retrieval and encounter scope mismatches \citep{wadden2022multivers,wadden2022scifactopen}. Surveys of this area identify evidence retrieval, domain transfer, rationale quality, and incomplete knowledge as persistent challenges \citep{vladika2023survey}.

Later benchmarks broaden the evidence modality and expose further limits. SciTab requires compositional reasoning over scientific tables, while SciVer evaluates multimodal evidence drawn from text, tables, and figures \citep{lu2023scitab,wang2025sciver}. MEVER jointly addresses multimodal retrieval, verification, and explanation over text and chart evidence \citep{zhang2026mever}. Recent systems such as SciTrue emphasize source-level traceability and inspectable attribution in LLM-assisted verification \citep{tan2026scitrue}. PhyVer moves beyond textual entailment by grounding material claims in multi-fidelity physical computations \citep{chen2026phyver}. Work on token-level uncertainty also shows that claim-specific uncertainty can help detect unreliable LLM outputs \citep{fadeeva2024factchecking}. This literature demonstrates that evidence proximity, retrieval, rationale selection, domain computation, and final authorization are distinct tasks.

ClaimBench does not compete with these systems as a textual verifier. Its SciFact adapter is intentionally transparent and weak. It is used to test whether an audit separates retrieval from authorization and abstains when the evidence is insufficient. More importantly, ClaimBench also audits claims whose evidence is not a document passage. A mechanistic statement may depend on a topology control, a directional analysis, an intervention, and a clean execution artifact. Textual entailment alone cannot decide whether those experiments were adequate. The LLM interface is consequently non-authoritative. It expands candidate coverage, but the decision remains bound to executable evidence.

\subsection{Non-compensatory decision support and uncertainty}
\label{subsec:noncompensatory-dss}

The decision-support literature has long examined how heterogeneous evidence should be assembled under incomplete or uncertain information. Software-supported evidence hierarchies make the sources and implications of uncertainty visible to decision makers \citep{davis2003evidence}. Multiple-criteria decision analysis distinguishes compensatory methods, where strong performance can offset a weak criterion, from non-compensatory methods with veto conditions. The ELECTRE family is a prominent example of the latter approach \citep{figueira2013electre}. ClaimBench adopts the same non-substitution principle but does not perform preference ranking. A missing causal or structural requirement vetoes only the claim that depends on it.

Abstention provides a related mechanism in machine learning. Selective prediction allows a model to withhold a decision and evaluates the trade-off between coverage and risk \citep{geifman2019selectivenet}. ClaimBench uses a three-state output for a different purpose. A claim is supported when its mandatory evidence is stable, blocked when it is absent, and uncertain when local perturbations change the decision. This uncertain state is an operational stability category. It is not a calibrated posterior probability or a conformal guarantee.

The distinction between compensation and veto is central to the proposed DSS. A scalar quality score is useful for ranking alternatives, but it can hide why a strong scientific interpretation fails. ClaimBench retains the criterion-level decision and returns an editorial action with provenance. This creates a direct link between decision theory and manuscript governance that is not provided by conventional model evaluation, assurance templates, or fact-checking benchmarks alone.

\subsection{Claim boundaries in neurocomputational modelling}
\label{subsec:neurocomputational-boundaries}

Mouse-brain modelling supplies the motivating application because its resources span incompatible scales. Mesoscale tracer data support region- and voxel-level connectivity models \citep{oh2014mesoscale,knox2019connectome}. The Virtual Mouse Brain uses connectome-based population dynamics for whole-brain simulation \citep{melozzi2017virtualmouse}. BMTK and the Allen V1 models support large neural networks at point-neuron and biophysically detailed resolutions \citep{dai2020bmtk,billeh2020v1}. These tools establish that simulation infrastructure and multiscale modelling already exist. A new simulator would not resolve which biological interpretation its output can support.

Predictive benchmarks create a different evidence layer. Sensorium and Dynamic Sensorium evaluate neural-response prediction for static images and videos \citep{willeke2022sensorium,turishcheva2023dynamic,turishcheva2024retrospective}. Foundation models extend prediction to new stimulus classes and population-level analyses examine what response geometry is preserved \citep{wang2025foundation,liscai2025population}. High predictive accuracy is valuable, but it does not uniquely identify synaptic topology, causal direction, or biological mechanism.

Connectomic resources add local structural evidence. MICrONS links electron-microscopy reconstruction with functional measurements over part of mouse visual cortex, and subsequent analyses examine wiring rules across visual areas \citep{bae2025microns,ding2025wiring}. These data can support controlled local structure--function analyses. They do not constitute a complete measured whole-brain connectome. At the opposite scale, Digital Twin Brain proposals and MouseDTB integrate inferred connectivity, spiking dynamics, data assimilation, and macroscopic validation \citep{xiong2023dtb,chen2026mousedtb}. MouseDTB is especially important because it demonstrates how far a whole-brain, single-neuron-resolution model can be developed. It also illustrates the claim-governance problem. Inferred neuron-level connectivity and agreement with BOLD signals are substantial modelling results, but their mechanistic and entity-specific interpretation still depends on identification assumptions and independent controls.

ClaimBench is positioned above these model families. It does not replace their data, simulators, or predictive systems. It asks which claim class each artifact supports and which stronger statements remain blocked. The novelty therefore lies in an executable evidence-to-wording decision layer, not in another mouse-brain model or another textual fact checker.
