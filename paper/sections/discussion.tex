\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The evaluation supports a specific methodological conclusion. Scientific AI claims can be represented as executable decision objects whose authorization depends on non-interchangeable evidence. The synthetic benchmark shows that compensatory and shortcut evaluators admit unsupported interpretations under the declared semantics. The external controls show analogous failure modes in public data. The release package shows that the resulting decisions can be connected to inspectable artifacts. None of these findings demonstrates that ClaimBench can replace peer review or determine scientific truth.

\subsection{Decision-support interpretation and practical use}
\label{subsec:decision-support-use}

The decision supported by ClaimBench is editorial and evidential. Given a candidate statement, the alternatives are to retain it, weaken it, mark it as uncertain, remove it, or request external judgement. The framework does not recommend accepting or rejecting a paper. It explains which mandatory evidence is present, which block failed, and which wording remains admissible. Table~\ref{tab:decision-actions} translates the machine-readable states into actions that an author or reviewer can apply.

In author-preparation mode, candidate statements are extracted from the manuscript and bound to the evidence package before submission. A blocked statement is revised at the point where the evidence gap is still actionable. In internal-review mode, a research group can compare alternative formulations against the same frozen artifacts. In reviewer-response mode, the contract exposes the exact criterion and result behind a disputed statement. These modes support a sequence of human decisions rather than one automated judgement.

The artifact binding is particularly important during revision. Numerical outputs can remain unchanged while the interpretation expands in the abstract, introduction, or conclusions. A conventional test suite will not detect that wording drift. ClaimBench treats public-facing text as part of the auditable system and records both permitted and blocked formulations. This does not prove that every unsafe paraphrase will be detected. It makes the expected boundary explicit and testable.

The LLM role follows the same DSS logic. A language model can increase candidate coverage and reduce the effort needed to inspect a long manuscript. It cannot create the experimental evidence required by a contract. Keeping extraction outside authorization also makes model replacement possible. Different deterministic rules or LLMs can propose different candidate sets while the evidence policy remains stable and reviewable.

\subsection{Lessons learned from the evaluation}
\label{subsec:lessons-learned}

The first lesson is that reproducibility and scientific identification are different properties. A clean package establishes that an analysis can be inspected and repeated under its declared environment. It does not show that the observed relation is unique, causal, or mechanistic. This distinction is easy to state and easy to lose when a paper reports one global quality narrative.

The second lesson is that non-compensation is useful only when claim requirements are defensible. Equation~\ref{eq:claim-gate} prevents one dimension from hiding another, but it cannot repair a poorly specified contract. Domain experts must justify why a block is mandatory, how it is measured, and which scope it covers. The synthetic result verifies implementation fidelity to those choices. It does not validate the choices independently.

The third lesson is that ORI and CNI must be read together. The topology-only evaluator has a low ORI because it authorizes few claims, but its CNI shows that it rejects most supportable opportunities. Conversely, correlation-only evaluation has greater coverage but unacceptable overclaiming. A useful claim DSS must expose this trade-off rather than optimize only for conservative behaviour.

The fourth lesson is that external controls should be selected by failure mode. SciFact is informative because it separates retrieval from support. Tuebingen is informative because it separates association from causal direction. Their task scores are secondary in this paper. This use of external data is modest but methodologically clearer than presenting weak baselines as competitive systems.

The fifth lesson is that a negative or uncertain decision can be a useful output. The threshold study shows that more than half of the tested configurations are dangerous, and the uncertainty experiment withholds support from locally unstable cases. A DSS that always produces a positive result would be poorly matched to claim governance. The value lies in identifying what additional evidence is needed and in preventing unsupported promotion.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}
\label{subsec:limitations}

The strongest limitation is external validity. The 144 known-truth cases are generated from the same operational semantics implemented by the gate. They provide a demanding software and decision-rule test, but they cannot establish that the taxonomy is complete or that the thresholds are scientifically correct. The five causal graph scenarios add structural diversity but remain synthetic. Broader known-truth generation, independent scenario design, and domain-expert adjudication are required.

The public controls are also bounded. The SciFact component is a local BM25 and lexical rationale baseline. Its purpose is to expose false support, and its errors cannot be generalized to modern scientific claim verifiers such as MultiVerS, SciVer, or SciTrue \citep{wadden2022multivers,wang2025sciver,tan2026scitrue}. The Tuebingen adapter combines simple directional proxies and performs below established causal baselines. It supports a causal abstention example, not a conclusion about the best causal-discovery systems.

The uncertainty analysis is deterministic and local. The values in Equation~\ref{eq:claim-stability} depend on a fixed perturbation magnitude and are not calibrated probabilities. The 243-cell grid is informative around the nominal thresholds but does not cover every interaction, evidence distribution, or dataset shift. Statistical calibration will require resampling, repeated datasets, and explicit error-control objectives.

The claim-discovery evidence is incomplete. The 120 candidates come from one frozen project corpus and were produced by deterministic patterns. No expert-annotated gold set is available, so extraction precision and recall are unknown. The absence of authoritative LLM calls strengthens reproducibility but does not demonstrate that an LLM-assisted configuration would remain stable across models and prompts.

Finally, the current study evaluates the decision artifact more strongly than its effect on people. It does not measure whether authors revise more accurately, whether reviewers agree more often, or whether the additional process reduces review time. These human outcomes are central for a DSS contribution. Until they are measured, the practical value of ClaimBench is supported by traceability and failure-mode analysis rather than by demonstrated improvements in editorial decision quality.

\subsection{Research agenda and future work}
\label{subsec:future-work}

The immediate next step is a blinded manuscript study. A corpus should contain scientific AI papers from several domains, expert-annotated claim spans, claim families, evidence requirements, and adjudicated wording decisions. ClaimBench, deterministic baselines, and fixed LLM extractors can then be compared on candidate recall, false authorization, abstention, inter-rater agreement, review time, and revision quality. This would test the DSS as a human-facing intervention rather than only as an executable framework.

A second line concerns calibration. Thresholds should be estimated on a development corpus and evaluated on independently held-out studies. Bootstrap or Bayesian models can quantify artifact-level uncertainty where their assumptions are appropriate. Selective-risk and conformal ideas may provide useful error-control objectives, but their guarantees cannot be transferred automatically to heterogeneous scientific claims. The target should be explicit control of false authorization at a declared coverage level.

A third line concerns contract design. The present taxonomy can be expanded to clinical validity, safety, fairness, deployment, and policy claims. This extension should not be a larger hand-written list. It should use expert elicitation, structured assurance patterns, and versioned domain profiles. Interoperability with assurance-case formats would allow existing safety and governance evidence to be referenced rather than duplicated.

A fourth line concerns provenance over time. Future releases should record contract versions, artifact hashes, data versions, environment manifests, and the exact text span authorized by each decision. A change in code, dataset, threshold, or wording could then invalidate only the affected claims. This would turn the current release audit into a longitudinal evidence-maintenance system.

The neurocomputational application remains useful for testing these extensions because it combines prediction, topology, direction, local structure--function evidence, simulation, and digital-twin language. MouseDTB and related models provide realistic high-scope claims, while Sensorium and MICrONS provide narrower predictive and structural evidence. A future domain study should ask whether independent neuroscience reviewers make better calibrated wording decisions when these artifacts are presented through ClaimBench. It should not assume that the present synthetic success already answers that question.
