\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This paper introduced ClaimBench, an executable framework for governing scientific AI claims. The framework separates evidence dimensions that are often conflated in applied AI papers, including prediction, reproducibility, topology, direction, causality, uncertainty, manuscript wording, cost, and release status. The non-compensatory design prevents strong evidence in one dimension from authorizing unsupported claims in another.

The current v2 package passes 15 of 15 reproduction stages, 10 of 10 unified report criteria, and 7 of 7 threat-model checks. External controls based on SciFact and the Tuebingen cause-effect benchmark show that the framework can audit claim-evidence behaviour beyond synthetic cases. The LLM-ready layer adds candidate extraction while preserving a strict boundary. LLMs can assist inspection, but executable artifacts authorize claims.

The defensible contribution is therefore not better prediction or causal discovery. It is a reproducible engineering layer that makes scientific claim boundaries explicit before publication. This is a narrower claim, but it is also a stronger one.

From a decision-support perspective, the main result is that ClaimBench turns manuscript interpretation into an explicit decision problem. The framework identifies candidate claims, maps them to evidence contracts, applies non-compensatory blocking rules, records uncertainty, and produces an artifact that can be inspected by authors and reviewers. This process is useful precisely because scientific AI papers often contain valid computational results whose interpretation still needs to be constrained.

Future work should proceed in three directions. First, the LLM interface should be evaluated with multiple fixed models, prompts, and blinded candidate extraction tasks while preserving the non-authoritative boundary. Second, the claim taxonomy should be extended to additional applied AI domains, including healthcare, industrial decision support, and high-stakes information systems. Third, human-in-the-loop studies should evaluate whether ClaimBench artifacts improve reviewer agreement, reduce unsupported wording, and make revision decisions more efficient.

The practical conclusion is conservative. ClaimBench should not be used to make a paper appear stronger than its evidence. It should be used to make the paper more precise. That is the reason why the framework is positioned as decision support for scientific AI claim governance rather than as a new predictive benchmark.
