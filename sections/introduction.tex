\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Computational neuroscience increasingly depends on reusable datasets, predictive models, connectomic reconstructions, simulation software, and derived statistical artifacts. These resources make analyses more powerful, but they also create a difficult knowledge-engineering problem. The evidence that supports one statement is frequently insufficient for another. Held-out response prediction does not identify the biological topology that generated the response. Repeated execution does not provide independent biological replication. A local structure--function association does not establish causal direction or whole-brain coverage. The technical problem addressed in this work is how to encode these distinctions as an executable authorization policy and how to verify that the resulting system does not silently promote a stronger claim.

Mouse-brain modelling is a demanding application because its public resources cover distinct biological scales. Allen tracer experiments describe mesoscale connectivity, population recordings characterize behaviour and neural response, Sensorium benchmarks measure predictive performance, and \ac{microns} links anatomy and function inside a restricted cortical volume \citep{oh2014mesoscale,willeke2022sensorium,turishcheva2023dynamic,bae2025microns}. The Virtual Mouse Brain, \ac{bmtk}, and detailed \ac{vone} models already provide established simulation capabilities \citep{melozzi2017virtualmouse,dai2020bmtk,billeh2020v1}. Digital Twin Brain proposals extend this landscape through assimilation and multiscale modelling \citep{xiong2023dtb,chen2026mousedtb}. None of these resources should be reduced to a common quality score, and a validation layer should not reimplement their simulators.

Existing scientific claim verification primarily retrieves documentary evidence and predicts whether a passage, table, or figure supports a proposition \citep{wadden2020scifact,wadden2022multivers,lu2023scitab,wang2025sciver}. Recent benchmarks also evaluate whether language models can extract and link claim--evidence pairs from complete articles \citep{javaji2025claimbench}. Assurance cases and knowledge-based systems provide explicit claims, supporting evidence, argument structures, and inference procedures \citep{buchanan1984rulebased,studer1998knowledge,mohamad2021assurance}. Provenance and graph validation are already standardized through \ac{prov} and \ac{shacl} \citep{lebo2013provo,w3c2017shacl}. These components are necessary but do not by themselves answer whether a package containing successful prediction, incomplete lineage, overlapping cohorts, or observational topology evidence authorizes a particular scientific interpretation.

The central design decision of MouseClaimBench is to separate structural conformance, package integrity, and domain authorization. Structural conformance asks whether the required fields and relations exist. Package integrity asks whether profile identity, content hashes, derivation references, independence declarations, and attestations are internally coherent. Domain authorization asks whether every mandatory evidence block for a bounded claim has passed its declared predicate. A package is authorized only when all three layers pass. Missing or failed requirements are returned together, so a topology deficit cannot be hidden by predictive performance and an invalid lineage cannot be hidden by an otherwise complete schema.

This paper develops the standards-aware authorization version of MouseClaimBench. It uses a strict profile-relative contract whose output is deliberately limited to \emph{authorized under profile $\Pi$} or \emph{not authorized under profile $\Pi$}, accompanied by the complete deficit set. This is not a probability that a scientific statement is true. It is a reproducible decision under one named policy whose content remains open to revision and external criticism.

\paragraph{Research contributions.}\label{sec:contributions}
The paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item A versioned authorization model that separates evidence-schema admissibility, scientific claim requirements, and non-compensatory decisions for computational mouse-brain studies.
\item A standards-aware evidence representation that exports \ac{rdf}/\ac{prov} and JSON-LD, together with independently executed \ac{shacl} shapes. Python, \ac{asp}, and \ac{shacl} produce identical decisions and complete deficit sets on 5,497 contract cases.
\item An integrity layer for content hashes, profile substitution, unresolved or cyclic lineage, duplicated evidence, overlapping cohorts, contradictory attestations, and missing block provenance. The layer is evaluated through single and pairwise attacks and component ablations.
\item A formal and empirical verification package covering authorization soundness and completeness relative to the profile, deficit identity, degradation monotonicity, input-order and irrelevant-evidence invariance, outside-profile closure, and measured scalability.
\item Two prospectively frozen applications to external \ac{dandi}/\ac{nwb} resources and a dependence-aware \ac{microns} application. Positive and negative outcomes are preserved without changing endpoints, thresholds, or selection rules after numerical access.
\end{itemize}

The claimed novelty is the evaluated composition of these elements for artifact-grounded scientific authorization. The work does not claim to invent assurance cases, graph validation, provenance ontologies, formal rule checking, or scientific fact verification. It also does not claim a consensus taxonomy, automatic peer review, biological truth, causal identification from observational data, or a complete digital mouse brain.
