\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Computational studies combine public data, fitted models, simulation engines, and automated analyses. Their conclusions are often described as predictive, reproducible, mechanistic, causal, or representative of a digital twin. These terms are not interchangeable. A model can predict held-out observations without identifying the process that generated them. An executable analysis can rely on a non-unique topology. A stable observational association can remain non-causal. The problem addressed in this work is how to encode these distinctions as executable knowledge and infer the strongest claim supported by a heterogeneous evidence package.

Mouse-brain modelling provides a demanding case. Atlases, tracer experiments, electrophysiology, connectomic reconstructions, response-prediction benchmarks, and simulation platforms operate at different scales \citep{oh2014mesoscale,melozzi2017virtualmouse,dai2020bmtk,billeh2020v1,willeke2022sensorium,bae2025microns}. Static or temporal prediction does not identify synaptic topology. A measured local synapse paired with a functional descriptor does not establish a whole-brain causal model. A brain-wide simulation based on inferred connectivity is not an entity-specific digital twin because it reproduces a macroscopic signal. Public availability and repeatable execution do not remove these evidence boundaries.

A global quality score cannot represent this situation reliably. Predictive correlation, cross-mouse agreement, matched structure--function differences, interventions, and clean software revisions have different meanings and units. Compensatory aggregation allows strength in one dimension to conceal a mandatory absence in another. Mechanistic wording should remain blocked when topology or direction fails, regardless of predictive performance. Causal wording requires intervention or an appropriate identification design, not reproducibility alone.

Knowledge engineering offers the required separation between a domain model, a fact base, an inference procedure, and an explanation \citep{buchanan1984rulebased,studer1998knowledge,guarino1998formal,lebo2013provo}. MouseClaimBench applies this structure to scientific claim authorization. A versioned profile defines ten mouse-brain claim types, their mandatory evidence blocks, and five prioritized rules. Evidence facts retain the original observations, executed predicate, rationale, source artifact, and revision. The engine returns supported, blocked, uncertain, out of scope, or requiring external review together with the decisive rule and its witnesses.

The inference policy is non-compensatory and deterministic for a fixed profile and fact package. Failed mandatory evidence takes precedence over review, missing evidence, and scope states. Support requires all mandatory blocks to pass. Candidate statements may be proposed by deterministic patterns or an optional \ac{llm}, but candidate discovery cannot create evidence or modify authorization. This boundary allows extraction technology to change without changing the scientific semantics of the decision.

\paragraph{Research contributions.}\label{sec:contributions}
The work provides four connected contributions: a validated and versioned mouse-brain claim profile, a provenance-preserving evidence fact model, a deterministic non-compensatory engine with proof traces, and a layered evaluation combining exhaustive states, independently labelled synthetic regimes, artifact-grounded cases, external controls, and clean release checks. Together they define a reproducible knowledge-based validation method rather than a global quality score.

Evaluation separates logical conformance from scientific evidence. Exhaustive tests cover all 625 state assignments for the four-block mechanistic claim. A structural-equation oracle provides 500 cases with independently defined graphs and interventions. Four artifact-grounded cases use Allen \ac{vbn}, Static Sensorium, Dynamic Sensorium, and \ac{microns}. SciFact and Tuebingen expose retrieval and causal-direction substitution failures \citep{wadden2020scifact,mooij2016cause}. The knowledge-engine audit also compares every inference with 40 frozen mouse-case decisions and verifies their proof traces.

Under the five oracle regimes, MouseClaimBench reduces the \ac{fpr} from 0.271 for a prediction shortcut to 0.019, while the \ac{fnr} rises from 0.007 to 0.070. It reproduces all 40 frozen real-case decisions and provides a complete trace for each one. The positive conclusions remain bounded to prediction, internal reproduction, topology specificity, and local structure--function association. No evaluated case supports causality, external biological replication, or a complete mouse-brain digital twin.

The contribution is therefore an evidence-constrained \ac{kbs}, not a new neural simulator, predictive model, causal-discovery method, or automated reviewer. Only the computational mouse-brain profile is evaluated. Section~\ref{sec:related-work} defines the context, Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the method, and Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}--\ref{sec:conclusions} report the evaluation and conclusions.
