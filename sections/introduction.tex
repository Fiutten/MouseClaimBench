\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Computational studies increasingly combine models, public datasets, simulation engines, and automated analyses. The resulting systems are commonly described as predictive, reproducible, explanatory, mechanistic, causal, or representative of a digital twin. These terms impose different evidence requirements. A model may predict held-out observations while failing to identify the process that generated them. An analysis may execute reproducibly while relying on a non-unique topology. A stable observational association may remain non-causal. The central problem addressed in this work is how to represent these distinctions as executable knowledge and how to infer the strongest claim supported by a heterogeneous evidence package.

Mouse-brain modelling makes this problem concrete. Public atlases, tracer experiments, electrophysiological recordings, connectomic reconstructions, neural-response benchmarks, and simulation platforms describe different biological scales \citep{oh2014mesoscale,melozzi2017virtualmouse,dai2020bmtk,billeh2020v1,willeke2022sensorium,bae2025microns}. Static or dynamic response prediction is not evidence that a synaptic topology has been identified. A measured synapse and a functional descriptor in a local cortical volume do not establish a whole-brain causal model. A brain-wide simulation based on inferred connectivity is not an entity-specific digital twin solely because a macroscopic signal is reproduced. These distinctions remain necessary when every dataset and program is publicly available.

The problem is not solved by assigning a single quality score. Predictive correlation, cross-mouse agreement, a matched structure--function difference, an intervention, and a clean software revision have different semantics and scales. Compensatory aggregation permits a strong result in one dimension to hide a mandatory absence in another. A mechanistic claim should not be recovered by prediction after its topology control fails. A causal claim should not be recovered by reproducibility when no intervention or causal-identification design was executed.

Knowledge engineering provides a suitable foundation. A \ac{kbs} separates a domain model, a fact base, an inference procedure, and an explanation of the resulting conclusions \citep{studer1998knowledge,buchanan1984rulebased}. Ontologies and structured vocabularies make the meaning of entities and relations inspectable, while provenance models preserve the origin and transformation of evidence \citep{guarino1998formal,lebo2013provo}. Scientific knowledge graphs have also been used to represent scholarly entities and generate testable hypotheses \citep{borrego2025research}. MouseClaimBench addresses a different operation. It does not generate a new hypothesis or predict textual entailment. It evaluates whether domain evidence satisfies the declared knowledge requirements of an existing claim.

This paper presents MouseClaimBench as an evidence-constrained \ac{kbs} for computational mouse-brain claims. A versioned profile declares ten claim types, the evidence blocks required by each type, and five prioritized inference rules. Evidence facts preserve source-scale observations, executable predicates, rationales, and artifact revisions. The engine produces one of five conclusions: supported, blocked, uncertain, out of scope, or requiring external review. It also returns a proof trace containing the fired rule, its witness facts, the rules evaluated before it, and the cryptographic identity of the profile.

The system is deliberately non-compensatory. Failed mandatory evidence has precedence over review, missing evidence, and scope states. Support is reached only when all required blocks pass. This ordering distinguishes a failed experiment from an experiment that was not performed. It also separates computational reproducibility, internal reproduction within one resource, and external replication across resources, laboratories, or studies. The policy remains deterministic for a fixed profile and fact package, even when an optional language model is used to discover candidate sentences.

Evaluation separates software conformance from scientific validity. First, a structural-equation oracle generates five regimes whose graphs and intervention status are defined independently of the evidence thresholds. Second, four real mouse-brain cases are reconstructed from Allen \ac{vbn}, Static Sensorium, Dynamic Sensorium, and \ac{microns} artifacts. Third, SciFact and Tuebingen expose retrieval and causal-direction failure modes outside the mouse adapters \citep{wadden2020scifact,mooij2016cause}. Fourth, the knowledge-engine audit compares every new inference with 40 previously frozen decisions and checks explanation completeness. The 144 constructed legacy cases remain regression tests because their labels share the operational semantics of the original gate.

The oracle benchmark shows the intended trade-off. The non-compensatory policy reduces the \ac{fpr} from 0.271 for a prediction-only shortcut to 0.019, while its \ac{fnr} increases from 0.007 to 0.070. The engine reproduces all 40 frozen real-case decisions and generates a complete trace for each one. The real cases retain bounded positive conclusions for prediction, internal reproduction, topology, and local structure--function association. They do not support causality, external biological replication, or a complete mouse-brain digital twin.

The contribution is a domain-specific knowledge representation and reasoning system, not a new neural simulator, predictive model, causal-discovery method, or automated peer reviewer. Its architecture can load another validated profile, but only the computational mouse-brain profile is evaluated here. Generality therefore concerns separation of knowledge, facts, rules, and explanations. It does not imply that mouse-brain predicates or thresholds transfer to another scientific domain.
